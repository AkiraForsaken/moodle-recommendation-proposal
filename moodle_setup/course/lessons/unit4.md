## KC013
video:
### Explanation
Modals of obligation show how necessary something is. **Must** = strong obligation, often the speaker's own rule or a written rule; **have to** = obligation from outside (a law, a boss); **should** = advice, a good idea. In the negative they differ: **mustn't** = it is not allowed; **don't have to** = it is not necessary.
### Examples
- Websites **must** protect users' data.
- I **have to** finish my article by six.
- You **should** check the date of a news story before sharing it.
### Common mistake
- ~~You mustn't buy a ticket; the show is free.~~ → You **don't have to** buy a ticket.
- ~~She musts go.~~ → She **must** go (no -s, no "to").
### Try it
- Choose: "Students ___ cheat in exams." (not allowed) => mustn't
- Choose: "You ___ register; anyone can comment." (not necessary) => don't have to

## KC014
video:
### Explanation
**May**, **might** and **could** show that something is possible but not certain. They are useful for careful opinions and predictions. **Might** often sounds a little less certain than **may**. Use the base verb after them, and **might not / may not** for negative possibility.
### Examples
- The interview **may** be published next week.
- This headline **might** confuse some readers.
- Prices **could** rise again in spring.
### Common mistake
- ~~It could to be true.~~ → It **could be** true.
- Using "can" for a specific possibility: ~~The minister can resign tomorrow.~~ → The minister **might** resign tomorrow.
### Try it
- Make it uncertain: "The festival will be cancelled." => The festival might be cancelled.
- Complete with a negative: "The rumour ___ be correct." => may not / might not

## KC015
video:
### Explanation
Verbs such as **think, believe, feel, suggest, argue** introduce opinions and are followed by a clause, often with **that**: "Critics **argue that** the law is unfair." For a negative opinion, English usually makes the verb negative: "I **don't think** it works", not "I think it doesn't work".
### Examples
- I **believe that** public libraries matter.
- The survey **suggests that** young people trust podcasts.
- I **don't feel** the ending was realistic.
### Common mistake
- ~~I am think that...~~ → I **think** that...
- ~~She suggest that~~ → She **suggests** that (third person -s).
### Try it
- Make a negative opinion: "I think the plan is good." (negative) => I don't think the plan is good.
- Complete: "Many readers ___ (feel) that the article is biased." => feel

## KC035
video:
### Explanation
Opinion phrases tell the listener that what follows is **your view**, not a fact: **In my view**, **From my point of view**, **Personally**, **I'd say that**, **To my mind**, **As I see it**. Put them at the start and follow most of them with a comma.
### Examples
- **Personally**, I prefer printed magazines.
- **To my mind**, the interview was too short.
- **As I see it**, the debate missed the main issue.
### Common mistake
- ~~In my opinion I think that...~~ (double opinion marker) → **In my opinion**, the show is...
- ~~According to me~~ → **In my view** / **Personally**.
### Try it
- Replace "I think": "I think the advert is clever." => In my view, the advert is clever.
- Is "Statistics show that..." an opinion phrase? => No, it introduces facts

## KC036
video:
### Explanation
Media words: a **headline** is the title of a story; a **byline** names the writer; a **column** is a regular opinion piece by the same writer; a **correspondent** reports from a place or on a topic; **breaking news** is happening now; to **broadcast** is to send out on TV or radio; a **subscription** gives regular access.
### Examples
- The **byline** says the story was written by Jo Grant.
- Our foreign **correspondent** is in Tokyo.
- The match was **broadcast** live.
### Common mistake
- Using "headline" for the whole article; it is only the **title**.
### Try it
- What is the line that names the writer of an article? => the byline
- What do we call news that is happening right now? => breaking news

## KC037
video:
### Explanation
Contrast markers connect two different ideas. **However**, **Nevertheless** and **On the other hand** start a new sentence and take a comma. **But** and **whereas** join two clauses in one sentence. **Although** begins a clause: "Although it is short, ...".
### Examples
- The plot is simple. **However**, the acting is excellent.
- Radio is local, **whereas** podcasts reach the world.
- The app is useful. **On the other hand**, it collects a lot of data.
### Common mistake
- ~~The film is long, however it is good.~~ → The film is long. **However**, it is good. (or: ..., **but** it is good.)
### Try it
- Join with "whereas": "Tom reads online. His parents buy newspapers." => Tom reads online, whereas his parents buy newspapers.
- Choose: "The show was popular. ___, it was cancelled." => Nevertheless / However

## KC058
video:
### Explanation
Tone is the writer's attitude. **Objective** texts report facts with neutral words. **Critical** texts use negative, strong words (disaster, failure). **Persuasive** texts try to make you act (We must..., Join us...). **Enthusiastic** texts use very positive words. Look at adjectives, imperatives and exclamation marks.
### Examples
- "Sales fell by 3% in May." → objective
- "This reckless plan will ruin our town." → critical
- "Don't miss it - book your seat today!" → persuasive
### Common mistake
- Judging tone by the topic: a sad topic can be reported objectively; check the **words**, not the subject.
### Try it
- "An outstanding, unforgettable performance!" What is the tone? => enthusiastic / strongly positive
- "Vote yes on Sunday and protect our parks." What is the purpose? => to persuade

## KC059
video:
### Explanation
A **fact** can be checked (numbers, dates, events). An **opinion** shows what someone thinks or feels and cannot be proved. Opinion signals: **should, best, worst, terrible, I believe, in my view**, and judgement adjectives (boring, beautiful).
### Examples
- Fact: "The bridge is 320 metres long."
- Opinion: "The bridge is the most beautiful in Europe."
- Mixed: "The museum opened in 2010, and it is wonderful." → first part fact, second part opinion.
### Common mistake
- Thinking that a number makes a sentence a fact: "90% of people **should** recycle more" is still an opinion.
### Try it
- Fact or opinion: "The festival has 40 stages." => fact
- Fact or opinion: "The festival is too expensive." => opinion

## KC060
video:
### Explanation
The **thesis** is the writer's main claim: what they want you to believe. It is often in the **first or last** paragraph and can usually be said in one sentence. Supporting reasons explain **why**; the thesis says **what** the writer argues.
### Examples
- "Schools should start at 9 am. Teenagers need more sleep, and..." → Thesis: Schools should start later.
- "In conclusion, the remake adds nothing new to the original." → Thesis of a review: the remake is not worth watching.
### Common mistake
- Choosing a supporting detail (a statistic, an example) instead of the overall claim.
### Try it
- "Cities need more cycle lanes. They reduce traffic and improve health." What is the thesis? => Cities need more cycle lanes.
- In "Teenagers need more sleep", is this the thesis or a reason in the example above about school times? => a reason

## KC082
video:
### Explanation
A thesis statement states your position clearly in one sentence. Formal stance markers make it strong and academic: **I would argue that**, **I firmly believe that**, **It is clear that**, **This essay will argue that**. Avoid vague words ("things", "stuff") and questions.
### Examples
- **I would argue that** online news needs stronger fact-checking.
- **I firmly believe that** museums should be free for students.
- **This essay will argue that** advertising in schools should be banned.
### Common mistake
- Writing a fact as a thesis: "Many people use social media." → no position. Better: "Social media does more harm than good to teenagers."
### Try it
- Make a thesis: "phones / classrooms / ban" => I would argue that phones should be banned in classrooms.
- Is "Do people read books?" a good thesis? => No, a thesis states a position, not a question

## KC083
video:
### Explanation
A concession admits a point against your view before you continue. Use **Although** / **Even though** + clause, then the main clause: "**Although** the article is long, it is easy to read." Do not add "but" to the same sentence. **Despite / In spite of** are followed by a noun or -ing form.
### Examples
- **Although** the series is popular, critics dislike it.
- **Even though** I enjoyed the book, the ending was weak.
- **Despite** the bad weather, the concert went ahead.
### Common mistake
- ~~Although it was cheap, but it was good.~~ → Although it was cheap, it was good.
- ~~Despite it rained~~ → **Although** it rained / **Despite the rain**.
### Try it
- Join: "The museum is small. It is fascinating." (Although) => Although the museum is small, it is fascinating.
- Correct: "Even though he trained hard, but he lost." => Even though he trained hard, he lost.

## KC084
video:
### Explanation
A review ends with a **verdict** and a **recommendation**: who should watch, read or buy it, and why. Signal the conclusion with **Overall**, **All in all**, **In short**, **To sum up**, then give a clear judgement and, if useful, a target audience.
### Examples
- **Overall**, this guidebook is excellent value for first-time visitors.
- **To sum up**, the app is fun but too expensive for most families.
- **All in all**, fans of the series will love this film.
### Common mistake
- Ending with a new detail instead of a verdict ("The film also has music.").
- Giving no recommendation, so the reader does not know your final view.
### Try it
- Write a short conclusion for a good, cheap restaurant. => Overall, I would recommend this restaurant to anyone looking for a good meal at a low price.
- Which phrase signals a conclusion: "Furthermore" or "To sum up"? => To sum up
